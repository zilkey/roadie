Night, day and stage mode

SoonSmall

The app gets used at noon in the sun and at 1am in a dark van. It should look right in both, and have a third look for reading a setlist on stage.

DayDefault in daylight. Light paper, dark ink.
NightDark, low glare, dimmed accents. Follows the phone, or switches at sunset.
StageBlack and yellow, huge type, screen stays awake. Setlist and run of show only.

How it works

  • Follow the phone's setting by default; override per person.
  • Optional "sunset" auto-switch using the show's city.
  • Stage mode opens from today's setlist, keeps the screen on, and blocks accidental edits.

To figure out

  • Does stage mode need a foot-pedal page turn (Bluetooth pedals send arrow keys)?
  • Big-type setlist for a music stand vs. a phone on a mic stand.

Principles: saves time and squinting (2). Cheap, since every color is already a token.

Dashboards with good vibes

SoonMedium

Most band stats feel like a report card. Ours tells the story of a band that's growing: more rooms, bigger crowds, better nights, and what to do next. Honest numbers, warm framing.

Shows this year4112 more than last year
Average crowd68up from 44
New cities7Missoula was the biggest
Paid to players$9,840everyone got paid, every time

Your Denver crowds doubled since spring. Lost Lake and Globe Hall both drew 80+. Worth pitching a headline night at Larimer Lounge?

What it shows

  • Shows, crowds and money per show over time, by city and by room.
  • Best rooms: pay, crowd, ratings together.
  • Listeners and followers from stat snapshots, next to when shows happened.
  • Milestones: 100th show, first sellout, first tour in the black.
  • A year-in-review card the band can share.

The vibe rules

  • Lead with what's growing; never shame a slow month.
  • Every number links to the shows behind it.
  • One suggestion at a time, and it's a next step, not a lecture.
  • Members see the non-money version: shows, crowds, cities, songs played most.

To figure out

  • How honest is "good vibes" when a tour loses money? Plan: say it plainly, then what would change it.
  • Comparing to other bands: probably never.

Principles: serves musicians (1) by showing progress they earned. Roadie can answer "how are we doing?" from the same numbers.

Show media folders

SoonSmall

Photos and videos from a show end up on six phones and nowhere else. Every show gets an Upload media button that makes a Drive folder for it and drops you right in.

How it works

  • First tap creates Band folder / Shows / 2027-03-12 Top Hat, shares it with everyone on the show, and opens it in Drive.
  • After that the button just opens the folder, with a count of what's in it.
  • Anyone on the show can use it, members and subs with the link included.
  • The post-show interview ends with "Got photos? Here's the folder."

Later, with Posting

  • The thank-you post picks its photo from this folder.
  • Roadie suggests the best few to post; a person picks.
  • Tour folders roll up every show on the tour.

To figure out

  • Big phone videos and Drive storage: it's the band's own Drive, so it's their space, not our bill.
  • Shows get media: {driveFolderId, url, count}.

Principles: one tap instead of a group-text scramble (2), and the files stay in the band's own Drive (1).

Songs from Drive, and Roadie with Sheets

SoonMedium

Most bands already have their songs as Word docs in a folder and their history in spreadsheets. Point the app at them and it brings everything in, instead of typing it all again.

Songs from a Drive folder

  • Point Songs at a folder of Word docs or Google Docs.
  • Each doc becomes a song: title from the file name or first line, key and tempo if the doc says them, the doc linked as its lyrics or chart.
  • Matches existing songs by title instead of duplicating them; shows a list to confirm before saving.
  • Re-run any time to pick up new docs.

Point Roadie at a Sheet

  • "Here's our old booking spreadsheet" with a Google Sheets link: Roadie reads it and offers to bring in venues, contacts and past shows.
  • "Update this sheet with this year's shows" writes back into a sheet you point it at.
  • Setlists from a sheet become setlists in the app.
  • Only the sheets you point at, picked with Google's file picker, so the app never sees the rest of your Drive.

To figure out

  • Messy sheets: Roadie proposes a column mapping and asks before importing.
  • A sheet you point at is data you handed over, like a pasted screenshot; it doesn't loosen Roadie's no-internet rule.

Principles: never make someone retype what they already have (2). The import goes through the normal commands, so it's signed and undoable.

Live performance royalties

LaterMedium

Songwriters can get paid when their songs are played live, but only if someone reports the setlist to their performing rights organization. Most bands never get around to it. We already know every song, every show and every setlist.

How it works

  • Songs get writers, their splits, each writer's organization (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC…) and the work ID once it's registered.
  • After each show, the setlist we actually played becomes a performance report: venue, date, songs.
  • A "Royalties" list shows which played shows haven't been reported yet, by organization.
  • Each report is prepared in the format the organization asks for, ready to submit through ASCAP OnStage or BMI Live.
  • The post-show interview asks "played the planned set?" so the report is right.

To figure out

  • Whether either organization offers a way to submit programmatically, or whether we prepare it and a person submits.
  • Each organization's deadlines and rules for which venues and shows qualify.
  • Registering new songs (works) as well, or just reporting performances.
  • Royalty amounts are the writers' business; the app tracks reported, not paid.

Principles: money musicians are owed and usually leave on the table (1), from data they already entered (2).

Venue calendars for opener slots

LaterMedium

The best support slots get booked months ahead. Pull in what's coming to the rooms we care about, three to six months out, and flag headliners we'd fit with, so we pitch before the bill is full.

How it works

  • A nightly server job reads each venue's public calendar feed (iCal, a listing API like Bandsintown's or Songkick's, or the venue's own feed).
  • Shows land in artists/{a}/venueEvents, linked to the venue.
  • Matching on genre, draw and how many openers the room usually books.
  • One tap makes a pitch for "support for {headliner}", aimed at the headliner's agent or the venue.
  • Home: "3 shows in the next 90 days you'd fit."

To figure out

  • Which sources allow this. Use official feeds and APIs, never scraping against a site's terms.
  • Roadie still can't browse the web; this is a server job with fixed sources, not an AI tool.
  • Shared with the Tenor venue list, if that happens.

Principles: saves hours of checking venue sites (2). Data comes in through one job we control, keeping Roadie's limits intact.

Posting: our Buffer

LaterLarge

Every show already has the date, room, time, ticket link and poster. Turn that into a posting schedule for every account, written in the band's voice, so promotion happens without a second app.

Feb 12Announce · Top Hat, Fri Mar 12, with Mae Sellers · poster + ticketsInstagram, Facebook, Bluesky
Mar 5Week of · "Missoula, one week" · rehearsal clipInstagram story
Mar 12Day of · doors 7, Mae at 8, us at 9All
Mar 13Thank you · crowd photo, "112 of you!"Instagram, Facebook

How it works

  • A schedule template per show and per tour: announce, week of, day of, thank you.
  • Roadie drafts the copy from the show's details; a person approves every post.
  • A queue and calendar across accounts, with the poster and clips from Drive.
  • Posts publish through each platform's official API where allowed; otherwise a reminder with the copy ready to paste.
  • Changes to the show (time moved, canceled) flag the posts that mention it.

To figure out

  • Platform APIs differ a lot in what they allow and cost; some need business accounts or app review.
  • Who connects accounts: each band, with tokens only that band's admins can use.
  • Post performance back into the dashboards.

Principles: one less paid subscription (1) and promotion from data you already entered (2). Sending stays with a person (3).

Seeing who else is pitching

SomedayMedium

Bands pitch the same rooms in the dark. If you choose to, let other bands see that you're going after a room or a weekend, so you can team up on a bill instead of competing for one.

How it works

  • Off by default. Per pitch, choose to share: band name, room, and the month, never notes, deal or contacts.
  • "Also pitching Lost Lake in January: The Low Tides. Want to pitch a shared bill?"
  • A board for "looking for an opener" and "looking for a headliner" by city and month.
  • Shared bills become one pitch with two bands on it.

To figure out

  • Bookers may not love it; frame it as better-built bills.
  • Abuse: a band claiming every date. Limits, and sharing ends when the pitch closes.
  • Needs enough bands in one city to be useful.

Principles: serves musicians as a community (1). Strictly opt-in, so nobody's booking is exposed.

Gear lists and a gear-sharing economy

SomedayLarge

Bands own a lot of gear that sits in closets, and touring bands need backline in towns they don't live in. Start with a proper inventory, then let bands lend and rent to each other.

Step 1: inventory

  • Every piece: what it is, who owns it, serial, value, photos, condition.
  • Feeds the show gear lists and an insurance list.
  • "What did we bring to Top Hat?" and "where's the DI box?"

Step 2: sharing

  • Mark pieces as lendable or rentable, with a calendar and a price you set.
  • Touring bands find a backline in Missoula from a band that lives there.
  • Check-out and check-in with photos; ratings both ways.
  • Money changes hands directly (Venmo or cash). We never take a cut.

To figure out

  • Damage and theft: deposits, insurance, or just trust between bands?
  • Tax and liability if it grows beyond friends.
  • Needs a lot of bands to work; start with lending inside a city.

Principles: no cut, ever (1). Starts useful for one band (the inventory) before it needs a crowd.
